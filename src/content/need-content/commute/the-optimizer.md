This is your fork, and getting it wrong is the most expensive ordinary mistake available to you. An extra twenty-five minutes each way is roughly two hundred hours a year — a month of working days — spent on the least redeemable activity there is.

### Decide the mode, not just the neighborhood

**Transit** wins if you work downtown, in Kendall, or anywhere on a strong rail spine. It is cheap, it does not care about traffic, and you get the time back as reading or podcasts rather than as vigilance.

**Biking** is the underrated answer and is often flatly the fastest for trips under about four miles, which covers a large share of Cambridge-to-Boston commuting. Its real advantage is variance: a bike commute takes the same time every day. Infrastructure has improved substantially and is still uneven — some corridors are protected, some ask you to be brave. Winter riding is a smaller minority here than in some cities, so plan a cold-weather fallback rather than assuming you will be a hero in January.

**Driving** is the worst of the three for a daily commute into the core and the best for anything that runs against the grain — a reverse commute out to 128, or an office in a suburban office park. Boston traffic is genuinely bad, the road network is medieval, and parking downtown is priced to discourage exactly what you are considering.

### The three hard cases

**Longwood.** The largest concentration of jobs in the region that the subway does not serve well. If you work here, prioritise living within biking or walking distance over almost every other housing consideration. People who commute in from the north spend years regretting it.

**The Seaport.** Very easy from South Station and Southie, meaningfully harder from Somerville, Cambridge, or anywhere north. The map flatters it.

**Anywhere requiring two transfers.** Two transfers is not 1.5x a one-transfer trip; it is roughly 2x, because you inherit the worst reliability of three legs and each connection has its own wait. Treat a two-transfer commute as disqualifying unless everything else is exceptional.

### Whether to have a car at all

For this mode a car is genuinely optional and the answer is situational. If you commute by transit or bike and want a car for weekends, run the numbers honestly: insurance in Boston proper is expensive, a resident permit entitles you to hunt for a space rather than to have one, street cleaning will move you regularly from spring through fall, and snow emergencies will move you in winter. Many people in this mode land on no car plus rentals, and are happier than the ones who kept it out of habit.
