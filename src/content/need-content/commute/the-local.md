No car. That is the mode, and Boston is one of the few American cities where it is not a sacrifice — the metro is small, dense, and old enough to have been built for feet.

The trade is specific and worth naming: you are giving up range in exchange for spontaneity. You will occasionally spend forty minutes travelling five miles on a route that looks absurd on a map. In exchange, everything within your radius is available to you at any moment without a parking decision attached, and no evening ever gets cancelled because you would have to move the car.

### What you will actually use

**Your feet**, for most of it. That is the point of picking the neighborhood the way you did.

**The bus**, more than you expect. Newcomers fixate on the subway map because it is legible, and then discover that the trips they make daily are crosstown ones the subway does not serve. Learn the two or three routes near you by number and check them before defaulting to a rail trip with a transfer.

**The subway** for downtown, the airport, and the cross-river trips. Four lines, colour-coded, plus the Silver Line, which is a bus that thinks it is a train. Reliability has improved meaningfully in the last couple of years after a long bad stretch, but it is still a system with old bones — build in a buffer for anything that matters and do not treat a late-night connection as guaranteed.

**Bluebikes**, the regional bikeshare, which covers Boston, Cambridge, Somerville, Brookline, and a widening ring beyond. It is the best answer to the awkward two-mile trip that is too far to walk and badly served by rail. An annual membership is cheap relative to everything else in this city.

### The parts nobody warns you about

Service thins out late. The last trains are earlier than you would expect from a city this size, which quietly shapes the social calendar — people leave bars at a time that will initially seem premature to you. Learn your last train, or accept that some nights end in a ride share.

Winter is a different system than summer. Snow, ice, and cold degrade everything: platforms are exposed, buses bunch, and the walk that is pleasant in September is a genuine consideration in February. Good boots are transit infrastructure.

### On not owning a car

You will occasionally need one. Rent it. Car share services, weekend rentals, and the friend with a Subaru all exist. Between insurance, a resident parking permit that only lets you compete for a space, excise tax, and the sheer overhead of moving the thing for street cleaning, owning a car you use twice a month is one of the more expensive habits available in this city.
