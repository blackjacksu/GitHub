Your version of friendship is the slowest to start and the hardest to lose. It does not come from meeting people. It comes from being *seen*, repeatedly, by the same people, in a place all of you have decided to keep going to — until one day someone says your name without you having introduced yourself again, and something quietly shifts.

Boston is good at this and bad at telling you so. The city has a reputation for being cold to newcomers, and the reputation is half-earned: a lot of people here have friend groups dating back to school, the student population turns over constantly enough that locals have learned not to over-invest in the new person, and the culture is reserved rather than effusive. What that produces is a place that is slow to open and, once open, unusually stable. The reserve is not rejection. It is a waiting period, and it responds almost entirely to one variable, which is showing up again.

### The method

**Pick two.** Not ten. A bar, a cafe, a court, a market, a branch library — two fixed places within walking distance. Exploration is the enemy here. Every new place resets your counter to zero.

**Go on the slow night.** This is the single most useful tactical detail on this page. On a Friday, the bartender is executing; on a Tuesday, the bartender is bored. The same is true of the market vendor, the cafe at 3pm, and the guy who always has the corner table. Choose the hour when the place has capacity for you.

**Sit at the bar, not at a table.** A table is a room you have rented. A bar is a bench you are sharing.

**Go back within a week.** Recognition decays fast and compounds slowly. Two visits seven days apart is worth much more than two visits a month apart, and the second visit is where nearly everyone quits.

**Say almost nothing for the first month.** You are not trying to make friends in week one. You are trying to become part of the furniture, and furniture does not introduce itself. The conversations arrive on their own once you have been there enough times to be a known quantity.

### The part that is genuinely hard

This takes about eight weeks to show any signal at all, and in that eight weeks you will feel foolish approximately every time. Sitting alone in a bar on a Tuesday when you know nobody is not a pleasant activity, and there is no version of this where it starts out feeling good.

But the thing you are building — a place that is yours because you kept going there — is the thing people spend four years accidentally stumbling into. Doing it deliberately is not cheating. It is just faster.

### Winter

Around January the city contracts. People stop lingering outside, plans get harder, and if your entire social infrastructure is outdoors you will lose most of it for three months. Make sure at least one of your two places is indoors and open in February. This is the month that decides whether people stay in Boston, and it is worth defending on purpose.
