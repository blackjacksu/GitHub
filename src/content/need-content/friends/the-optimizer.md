You are efficient about most things and this is the one place efficiency actively hurts you, because friendship does not respond to optimisation. It responds to time, and specifically to *unstructured* time, which is the resource your whole life is arranged to minimise.

So the move is not to try harder. It is to choose formats where the recurrence is somebody else's job.

### Buy the calendar, not the activity

A rec league, a ten-week class, a run club with a fixed night, a climbing gym you attend on the same two evenings. What all of these have in common is that the schedule already exists, the same people return, and nobody — critically, not you — has to organise anything.

This matters because the standard adult failure mode is the group chat where everyone agrees they should really hang out more and then nothing happens for four months. Someone has to do the work of scheduling, that someone is usually nobody, and the friendship starves. A league solves this by taking the decision away from all of you.

Boston is well set up for this. Adult rec leagues run in almost every sport, the class ecosystem is deep, and the free run and ride clubs are numerous enough that you can find one on any night of the week. Pick on the basis of what night you can reliably protect, not on the basis of what sounds most appealing. The night is the constraint.

### The second-thing rule

Here is the specific behaviour that separates people who build a real circle from people who accumulate friendly acquaintances for years:

After you do a thing with someone, propose a *different* thing.

You played soccer together — get a beer. You got a beer — ask if they want to come to the thing on Saturday. Each of these moves the relationship out of a single context, and a friendship that exists in two contexts is dramatically more durable than one that exists in a single recurring slot. Almost nobody makes this move, because it requires a small amount of exposure. It is the highest-yield social act available to you and it costs one text message.

### The work trap

Your job is good, your colleagues are smart, and the path of least resistance is for your entire social life to be people from work. This is worth resisting for one reason: it is contingent. When you change jobs — and in this region's tech, bio, and academic worlds, you will — a purely work-based social life largely evaporates, and you restart at thirty-four instead of at twenty-six.

Keep at least one recurring thing that has nothing to do with your employer. One is enough. It just has to be independent.

### On Boston specifically

The professional world here is small and interconnected in a way that is unusual for a city this size. People move between the same set of institutions and remember each other. This is an advantage for your career and a mild hazard for your social life, in that the two can collapse into each other without you noticing. Keep one thing separate.
