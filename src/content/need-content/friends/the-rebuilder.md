This is the whole job. Everything else on this site is logistics; this is the thing you actually came here to solve, and it is the only need where the honest answer is a behaviour rather than a list.

Start from the real constraint: adult friendship requires repeated unplanned contact with the same people, and adult life supplies almost none of it. School and university manufactured it — same building, same hours, same faces, for years. Nothing after that does it for you. So it has to be built on purpose, and the built version works exactly as well as the accidental one. It just feels artificial while you are doing it.

### The protocol

**Pick two recurring things.** Two, not one, because one can die from a schedule change. Not five, because five is a way of never becoming a regular at any of them. Ideally different shapes — one physical, one not; one where you talk, one where you do not have to.

**Go four times each before you judge it.** This is the part that matters and the part nearly everyone skips. Here is why four:

- *Visit one*: you are a stranger. Nobody remembers you. It feels like nothing happened, because nothing did.
- *Visit two*: one or two people register that they have seen you before. Still no conversation. This is where almost everyone quits, and quitting here is the single most common reason people conclude that a city is unfriendly.
- *Visit three*: someone says hello first. That is the signal. It is small and it is the whole thing.
- *Visit four*: you have a role — you are the new guy who plays defence, the one who knows the geography questions, whoever. A role is much easier to occupy than a personality.

Nothing about that sequence is Boston-specific. What is Boston-specific is that the reserve here makes visits one and two feel colder than they would elsewhere, which makes quitting more tempting, which is why the four-visit rule is worth writing down and holding yourself to.

**Then make the out-of-format ask.** Around visit four or five, ask one person to do something *not* at the thing. This is the step that converts a recurring acquaintance into a friend, and it is a real risk — sometimes people say no, and that is survivable, and you should do it anyway.

### Say yes indiscriminately, at first

For the first three months, go to things you are not excited about. Accept the invitation from the person you are unsure about. Attend the party where you will know one person.

This is inefficient by design. You do not yet have enough information to filter well, your instincts have been calibrated in a different city with a different version of you, and the cost of a mediocre evening is a mediocre evening. After three months you will have enough signal to be selective, and then you should be. Doing it in the other order — being selective first, opening up later — is how people spend a year alone.

### It will feel forced

For roughly six weeks this will feel like a project rather than a life. You will be aware of doing it. There will be evenings you go out specifically because a document told you to, and that will feel like a slightly embarrassing thing to be doing.

Then, without a clear moment, it stops. Someone saves you a seat. You get added to a group chat. You go because you want to see people rather than because it is on the plan. The scaffolding comes down and what is left is just your life.

The person who built this site took four years to get there without a plan. The plan is not a shortcut around the work. It just stops you from spending three of those years doing visit one over and over.
