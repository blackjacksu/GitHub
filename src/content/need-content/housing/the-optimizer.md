Your housing decision is downstream of one number: how long it takes you to get to work, door to door, at the hour you will really leave. Everything else is negotiable. So do this in the correct order — draw the constraint, then shop inside it — because the failure mode of this mode is falling for an apartment and then rationalising a fifty-minute commute you will resent by November.

### Where you work changes the answer completely

Boston is not a city with one commute problem. It has several, and they barely overlap.

**Kendall and the rest of Cambridge** is the easiest to solve. The Red Line north of downtown is the strongest transit spine in the region, so Davis, Porter, Central, and Inman all work, and biking is genuinely competitive over those distances.

**Longwood** is the hardest, and people underestimate it for years. It is a dense medical and research district that the subway serves awkwardly, which is why so much of its workforce funnels through a small number of buses and the Green Line E branch. Living where you can bike or walk to Longwood is worth a real rent premium. Brookline, Mission Hill, JP, and the Fenway edge are the honest shortlist.

**The Seaport** looks close to South Station on a map and is close to it in practice, so downtown-adjacent and Southie both work. It is less well served from the north and west than its skyline suggests.

**Downtown or the Financial District** is the one case where almost everything works, which means you should optimise for something other than the commute.

### The two-stop question

Rent in Greater Boston falls off with distance from the core in a way that is steep and fairly legible. Two stops further out is frequently a few hundred dollars a month. Two stops further out is also fifteen minutes each way, which is about a hundred hours a year.

There is no universally right answer, but there is a wrong way to decide it, which is to decide it implicitly. Put the number on both sides and pick.

### Things that will surprise you

The housing stock is old — triple-deckers and converted two-families dominate — so central air is rare, radiators are common, and "renovated" covers an enormous range. Laundry in the building is a real amenity here, not a given. And if you are bringing a car, ask about the parking spot in the same breath as the rent; in the denser neighborhoods a deeded spot can cost as much as a small bedroom.
