You are optimising for departure. The apartment is a basecamp: somewhere to dry things out, store a rack of gear, and sleep between trips. Which means the ordinary Boston housing priorities — steps to the T, walkable main street, a bar you like — matter less to you than they do to almost anyone else, and two things matter far more.

### One: off-street parking

Not "there's usually street parking." Off-street. A driveway, a deeded spot, a lot behind the building. In the dense neighborhoods, on-street parking is a resident-permit system per neighborhood, and the permit gives you the right to compete for a space rather than the right to have one. Add street cleaning bans from spring through fall, and the winter parking ban during snow emergencies, and you get a recurring low-grade tax on your attention that is worst exactly when you are trying to leave at 6am with a loaded car.

Paying more for a spot is the single best trade available to you.

### Two: how long it takes to get on the highway

Your radius is set by three roads. **Route 93 north** is the Whites and most of northern New England. **Route 2 west** is Concord, the Berkshires, and the quieter way toward Vermont. **The Pike** is west and, via 495 and 24, most of the way south. If you live somewhere that reaches one of those in under fifteen minutes, your two-and-a-half-hour radius is real. If it takes you thirty-five minutes to clear the city, it is not, and you will start skipping trips without ever deciding to.

That geometry is why Arlington, Watertown, Medford, and Malden are on your list ahead of much more famous neighborhoods. They are unglamorous, they are cheaper, they have driveways, and they are the first exit off the city.

### The trade you are making, said out loud

You are giving up walkability. In practice that means most weeknights you will drive to anything you do, and your neighborhood will not accumulate familiar faces on its own the way a dense one does. That is a real cost and the mode does not pretend otherwise — see the Friends section, which is where this bill comes due.

### One thing not to skip

Somewhere to put wet, salty, sandy gear. New England is four seasons of things that need to dry. A basement, a mudroom, a porch, a garage — anything. People who do not solve this end up with a hallway full of equipment and a slow reluctance to go out again.
